You leave with three things that work

Not exercises that get thrown away on Friday. Each of these still runs after you go home, and you understand it well enough to change it.

a website, on the internet

A site at your own address

You describe the site you want, then put it on a real server with a real domain and a real certificate. You will break it at least once and put it back, which is the part that teaches you something.

a tool you keep

Something that reads your invoices

Point it at a folder of supplier PDFs, each one laid out differently, and get back a single tidy spreadsheet. You build it, then you make it survive an invoice it has never seen before.

a method

A way through a pile of documents

Contracts, receipts, scans and photographs, turned into a summary with every claim traced back to the page it came from. Useful the week you return.

Whether this is your course

Come if

  • You have work that is obviously repetitive, obviously automatable, and nobody to hand it to.
  • You can describe what you want precisely, in English.
  • You are willing to read what the computer did before you agree to it.
  • You have a laptop you are allowed to install things on.

Skip it if

  • You write software for a living. You will be bored by lunch on day one.
  • You want a certificate more than you want a working tool.
  • You are hoping to approve the output without reading it. That is the one habit this course will not give you.
  • Your employer blocks command-line tools and will not make an exception.

The three days

Day one

Asking for things well

The terminal, and why it stops being frightening by about eleven o’clock. How to describe a task so that the answer is the one you wanted. Reading a plan before you approve it, and saying no.

Day two

Building it, and putting it somewhere

From an empty folder to a site other people can open. Servers, certificates and domains, handled by description rather than by memorised commands. Undoing a bad change calmly.

Day three

Your own work, on your own files

You bring the job you actually want done. You leave having built it, tested it against the awkward cases, and written down enough that a colleague can run it without you.
